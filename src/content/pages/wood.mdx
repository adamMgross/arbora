You hold what you need to see, follow three lights into the dark, and come out with a tree, a presence, and a passage. The question is never typed. The wood answers questions about the one who walks in.

<Deck deck="wood" />

<h2>The three circles</h2>

Fifty-two cards in three circles, which are also the three positions of every walk. **Elders** are sacred trees across traditions: the ground you stand on, roots, lineage, what holds. **Spirits** are the beings and presences of the wood: what meets you, the otherness of the forest, what moves. **Passages** are what happens to a person among trees: what is happening, the process the wood is working on you. Each walk draws one of each, so a reading has a grammar without needing a separate spread.

The setting is the North American conifer forest. The walk climbs as it darkens: the coast's cedar and fir in mist, the mountains' spruce and aspen, the north woods' fir and birch. The language comes from everywhere. The trees come from here.

<h2>How to use it</h2>

Read the one line at the door and hold a question about yourself. Walk in. If you kept a line last time, the wood shows it and asks what came of it. Follow a light, and the Elder turns over on the left wing. Follow a second, and the Spirit turns over on the right. Follow a third, and the Passage opens the center. Look before the book. Open it: three stories, three passages, one question. Keep one line, or leave it on a branch, after the omikuji tied to a pine, and it will never be shown again. The wood closes until tomorrow.

For the table, a switch at the door draws one Passage and turns its question into a prompt for the weekly gathering. Nothing is saved from a table draw.

<h2>Care with living traditions</h2>

Many of these stories belong to nations whose people are alive and practicing. The cards draw only from stories those nations have shared publicly, name the nation every time, and prefer Native-authored sources. No ceremonial objects or restricted ceremonies appear.
