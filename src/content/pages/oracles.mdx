These are mirrors, not telescopes. Nothing here predicts. An oracle, in every tradition that kept one, was a way to stop, hold a question, give up the choice of what comes next, and read what came with care. The three decks below keep that shape and share one spine: you hold, you cross a threshold, you cede the draw, you look before you read, you open the book, you keep one line, and you return to it.

They are built to feel different from each other. The Wood is pure chance, one card or three in three positions, for the day you are lost. The Sky draws by chance and then reads the real heavens standing over California at this minute, as symbol, setting each plate where its light stands and asking what it asks of you. The Year is given by the date, with one card drawn beside it, and keeps a notebook that comes back every year.

<h2>What they share</h2>

Each deck can be drawn as often as you like, and every draw begins again at the threshold. Each keeps what you write on this device only, in your browser, and never sends it anywhere. Each lets you set a hard reading down instead of keeping it. And each draws its passages from the traditions the canon already holds, with the source named every time.

<h2>Where they came from</h2>

The structures are old. The held question that is never spoken aloud comes from the Ifá diviners of West Africa, who do not hear the question before they cast. The record you return to is the Shang practice of writing the outcome beside the oracle. The fixed symbol set with a growing body of verses is how the I Ching and the 256 odu have worked for millennia. The leaving of a bad reading on a branch is from the omikuji tied to pines at Japanese shrines. The seven heavens are the ladder that Inanna, the Hermetic books, and Dante all climbed. The seventy-two windows are Japan's old calendar, renamed for the California year.
